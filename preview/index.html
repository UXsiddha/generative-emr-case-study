01Product overview

What if the EMR could listen to the doctor, and fill itself in?

An assistant that sits beside the medical record doctors already use. Doctors type or talk normally, and it fills in the record’s fields as they go. The record stays the same; only the way it gets filled in changes.

02The problem

The form was never the problem. The typing was.

The problem was filling in the form by hand, not the form itself.

In most medical record systems, doctors type every detail into fixed fields, during or after the visit. The form itself was fine: the fields were the right ones, and no doctor wanted a different form. What wore them down was typing it all by hand, between patients, all day.

03Constraints and design principles

The goal was not to redesign the system, but to improve it without disrupting it.

The principle, agreed before any design decision

Improve the record without changing what the record is.

  1. 01 · The system

    An existing technical system

    We were not starting from scratch. The system was already live and built in-house, so any design that could not be built was spotted straight away.

    Led toWorking prototypes, not static screens.

  2. 02 · The doctors

    High-intensity users

    Doctors are short on time and push back on anything that changes how they already work. Even small annoyances make them drop a tool.

    Led toAn assistant that sits beside the record; the doctor never has to click into a field.

  3. 03 · The product

    A core that had to stay put

    Rewriting the structure would solve a problem nobody had, and create one everybody would feel.

    Led toThe pivot away from a standalone platform.

Agreeing on this early ruled out the most ambitious version of the idea, and gave the team a test to check its own work against later. That is exactly what happened.

Get clear early. Check what can be built, fast. Decide using working prototypes.

  1. HypothesisAI can reduce documentation effort.
  2. MapEvery field and step in the current record, and every point where a doctor repeated themselves or switched screens.
  3. BoundTurn those moments into a few clear, limited jobs for the AI.

Three use cases

  1. 01Fill in the record’s fields automatically from what the doctor types or says.
  2. 02Notice when a new symptom or diagnosis needs a new section in the record.
  3. 03Read a doctor’s history and background to produce better templates.

Most tools generate notes. This one generates the record itself.

04How the idea changed

The version that demoed best was the one we dropped.

  1. WhiteboardingStage 1 · Explored

  3. The reversalStage 3 · Dropped
  4. The generative layerStage 4 · Built
  5. PersonalisationStage 5 · Built

The first answer

A separate app built around chat, on the same database, with tabs copied from the record’s old side menu.

Whiteboarded, then drawn as a full journey covering patient overview, detailed record and consultation start. It looked like the confident version of the idea.

Why it was dropped

It partly went against the principle we had agreed on, improving the system without changing its core, and raised real questions about whether it could be built.

Note what that does not say. Not that it was wrong, or that users rejected it. It was judged not to fit a principle we had agreed on beforehand.

06Three key design decisions

The record stays. The typing goes.

  1. The doctor

    “Temperature 100.4, pulse 96, BP 128 by 84…”

    Types or talks normally, in the assistant next to the record.

  2. Decision A · Input
    Voice and chata recording the doctor can replay

    Natural input, captured where the doctor already works.

  3. The layer
    Writes the recordstructured, in real time

    Smart fill adds details the doctor never mentioned.

  4. Decision B · Readback
    Says what it wrotefield by field, reviewed before use

    Never silently populating the form.

  5. Decision C · Shape
    Arrives shapeda template from the doctor’s profile

    Editable by chat, voice or hand.

Decision A

Natural input into structured records.

Problem
Doctors type every detail into fixed fields, during or after the visit.
Rationale
The record stays as it was; the assistant sits beside it, and the doctor never has to click into a field.
Design
Doctors record the conversation or type, and the record fills itself in from what was said. Smart fill goes a step further and adds sections the doctor didn’t mention.
Value
It changes nothing about the record, and everything about filling it in.

Decision B

It shows what it filled in, and the doctor checks.

Problem
The reminder to check AI output only works if doctors can tell what the AI filled in from what they typed.
Rationale
The assistant lists what it wrote, instead of quietly filling the form. The doctor can replay what was said, which matters because the AI wrote into a medical record based on it.
Design
A list of every field it filled, a recording the doctor can replay, and a reminder at the bottom of every assistant screen.
Value
Checking is not a hurdle; it is the product taking its own warning seriously.

All outputs are intended to assist and should be reviewed before use.

Decision C

Personal templates and quick actions.

Could the record arrive already shaped for the person opening it?

A cardiologist opens to

Cardiac risk profile
Smoking history, family history of heart disease (CAD), high cholesterol (dyslipidemia).
Ejection fraction and echo
How well the heart pumps (LVEF), and signs of damaged heart muscle (RWMA).

“Based on your profile as a Cardiology specialist, I’ve pre-configured your EMR with your preferred sections.”

The assistant says so. Declared, not silent, and editable by chat, voice or hand.
Problem
The record already shipped with templates for Cough, Cold and Fever, picked by hand from a row of buttons.
Rationale
This does the same job, without the picking.
Design
When a doctor first signs up, it reads their background from the company’s existing doctor database and builds a personal starting template they can fully edit.
Tested
Against four doctors’ data.

A working surface, not a chat window

Type “/” to book appointments, pick a date on a calendar inside the chat, tap suggested next steps, and hide the panel to see the whole record.

07The complete consultation

One consultation, start to finish, in one chat.

In the assistant panel

A welcome that lists what it can do, a voice recording the doctor can replay, a list of every field it filled, booking the next visit in the chat, and a summary to share at the end.

Unchanged in the EMR

Same tabs, same medication table, same examinations row, same footer actions. Nothing in the record changes; the assistant only fills it in.

  1. 01

    Session opens

    The assistant opens with four capabilities: manage appointments, create and complete prescriptions, turn conversations into EMR notes, retrieve patient details.

  2. 02

    Doctor speaks

    Voice capture running, beneath the fields the assistant has already written.

  3. 03

    Record fills, then the next visit

    The assistant lists the fields it wrote, and the doctor sets the next appointment in the same chat.

  4. 04

    Consultation ends

    Prescription generated, with share, invoice and follow-up offered.

Decision
Nothing requires leaving the conversation. Recording, booking, the summary and sharing all happen in one chat.
Achieved
A consultation is documented, scheduled and shared from a single chat.

09What I learned

Three things this project settled about designing for AI.

  1. On prototyping AIIntelligence cannot be drawn, only run.

    A static screen can claim the system understood. It cannot show it understanding. On AI work the prototype is the design, not a picture of it.

  2. On adding to an enterprise productThe shift was structural, not cosmetic.

    The gain came from changing how information gets into the record, not from redrawing it. Every screen we left alone was part of the result.

  3. On constraintsWrite the constraint down before the work.

    One sentence, agreed in advance, is what let us kill our own best-looking direction. A principle only works if it exists before the thing it has to judge.

10What comes next

The measures that matter.

  1. 01What share of prescriptions get generated through conversation rather than typing.
  2. 02Whether the time from patient to completed record actually falls.

Both are still to be measured. The usage above is the only evidence so far.

Keep the structure clinicians already rely on. Rethink how information gets into it.
